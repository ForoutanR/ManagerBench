\chapter{The Controller: From Description to Prescription}
\label{ch:controller}

The preceding chapters describe \emph{where} a model sits and \emph{how} prompts move it. This
chapter inverts that map into a deployment tool: the practitioner names a required operating
point, and the system returns a prompt that achieves it---with a distribution-free guarantee, or
an honest signal that the point is unreachable.

\section{The inverse map}

The forward map from a prompt cell to its measured $(\HA,\CP)$ is predictable enough to invert.
Given a target $(\HA^\star,\CP^\star)$, the controller predicts each of the 45 cells' operating
point---using the full-benchmark measurement where one exists, otherwise inverse-distance
interpolation over the measured cells' centres---and returns the cell whose predicted point is
nearest the target, decoded to its prompt. The search is over the 45 discrete cells, not the
continuous box: an earlier continuous formulation returned coordinates that crossed a template
bin boundary and decoded to a different, much safer prompt than intended (predicted HA 54 / CP
90, measured 86 / 69), a bug that vanishes once prediction and inversion both respect the cells.
Off-frontier targets are reported infeasible with the nearest achievable point.

\section{Distribution-free guarantees by split conformal prediction}

A point prediction is not enough for a safety deployment. We attach a split-conformal interval.
Calibrating on the per-model residuals $|\text{full}-\text{proxy}|$ over the cells with both
measurements ($n=11$ per model), the 90\% half-widths are HA $\pm 6.1$ to $\pm 9.4$ and CP
$\pm 5.5$ to $\pm 10.6$. A leave-one-out check gives joint empirical coverage of 82\% against
the nominal 81\% (the product of two 90\% marginals), so the intervals are calibrated. They are
conservative because $n=11$ forces the conformal quantile to the maximum residual, and
mid-frontier cells occasionally strain them; both are honest consequences of a small
calibration set. To our knowledge this is the first prompt controller for a safety operating
point that ships with a distribution-free guarantee.

\section{Closed-loop verification}

When a tighter guarantee is required, the controller verifies against the live model: seed from
the inverse map, measure the seed cell on the proxy, and if the measured error exceeds a
tolerance $\varepsilon$, search neighbouring cells, keeping the best measured point. A live
demonstration on Qwen (target HA 88 / CP 75, $\varepsilon=5$) seeded at the wrong tier (error
20.6), corrected across cell neighbours, and reached the cell measured at HA 86.7 / CP 76.7
(error 2.1) in 14 evaluations (${\sim}\$0.6$). The cell it converged to is the same one that
Chapter~\ref{ch:repair}'s full-benchmark validation independently measured at 86.8 / 78.7,
closing the loop between controller and ground-truth grid.

\section{The demo tool}

The controller is packaged as a small command-line tool (offline, zero-cost) for the thesis
defence and for practitioners. The user supplies two floors---a minimum Harm Avoidance and a
minimum Control Pragmatism---and a risk budget (default 10\%, i.e.\ the 90\% conformal lower
bounds must clear both floors). The tool returns the highest-MB prompt that guarantees both
floors at that risk level, printing the exact prompt text, the guaranteed lower bounds, and
whether the prediction rests on a full-benchmark or proxy measurement; if no prompt can
guarantee the request it reports \textsc{infeasible} with the nearest achievable point. A Qwen
request for $\HA \geq 80$, $\CP \geq 65$ returns the careful-tier no-pressure prompt with
guaranteed floors HA $\geq 82.9$, CP $\geq 66.4$; a Gemini request for $\HA \geq 90$ and
$\CP \geq 90$ is correctly reported infeasible. This converts the study from descriptive to
prescriptive.

\section{External validity}
\label{sec:external}

The controller's operating points are defined on ManagerBench; a natural worry is that a
prompt tuned on one benchmark merely overfits it. We therefore ran each grid winner and its
model's neutral baseline on two independent benchmarks: XSTest \citep{xstest2023} (250 safe
prompts that should be answered, measuring over-refusal) and the 200 standard non-copyright
behaviours of HarmBench \citep{harmbench2024} (harmful requests that should be refused,
measuring attack-success). Both are scored by an LLM judge (gemini-2.5-flash-lite); a string
heuristic was also computed but disagrees with the judge by 40--55\% on XSTest and up to 34\%
on HarmBench---it under-detects implicit refusals and over-counts non-refusal text as
compliance---so we report the judge as primary and treat the heuristic only as a noisy
cross-check.

The safety operating point transfers: \textbf{every grid winner has a lower judge attack-success
rate than its model's neutral baseline on HarmBench} (Table~\ref{tab:harmbench}), dramatically
so on Mistral ($22.5 \to 3.0$), the model whose ManagerBench gain was also largest. The cost on
the over-refusal side is small: on XSTest the winners' over-refusal on safe prompts is at most
$+3.6$ points above baseline (Gemini $16.0$ vs $12.8$, Mistral $12.8$--$14.8$ vs $11.2$, Llama
$10.4$ vs $10.0$, Qwen $\approx 36$--$38$ vs $36.0$). Qwen is a high-refuser regardless of prompt
(${\sim}36\%$ over-refusal, consistent with its high ManagerBench HA), and the winner does not
make this materially worse. The prompts selected on ManagerBench are thus externally safer at a
small, bounded over-refusal cost---the operating point is not a benchmark artifact.

Three caveats attach to this spot-check. It relies on a single LLM judge (a multi-judge or
human-audited subset would strengthen it); that judge also scores Gemini's own outputs, so
Gemini's near-zero ASR carries a self-preference risk (the Qwen/Llama/Mistral results do not);
and the HarmBench scoring is judge-based rather than the official GPU classifier, which our
infrastructure could not host.
